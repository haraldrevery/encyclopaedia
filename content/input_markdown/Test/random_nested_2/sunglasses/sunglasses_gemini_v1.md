# Sunglasses

## Overview
Sunglasses are protective eyewear designed primarily to prevent bright sunlight and high-energy visible light from damaging or discomforting the eyes. In survival and emergency preparedness scenarios, they are critical equipment for preventing ultraviolet (UV) damage, reducing eye fatigue, preventing debilitating conditions like snow blindness (photokeratitis), and providing a physical barrier against wind, dust, and debris.

---

## Usage & Safety Protocols

### How to Use
1. **Inspect Before Use:** Check the lenses for deep scratches that might impair vision or compromise the UV coating, and ensure the hinges are secure.
2. **Clean Lenses:** Use a microfiber cloth and lens cleaner (or clean water) to remove smudges or grit. Never wipe with a dry, dirty shirt, as this causes micro-scratches.
3. **Position:** Place the sunglasses on your face, ensuring the nose pads or bridge sit comfortably. The frames should be close to the face to minimize light entering from the top and sides.
4. **Secure:** For active or survival scenarios, attach a retention strap (eyewear retainer) to the temples to prevent dropping or losing them.
5. **Removal:** Remove using both hands on the temple arms to prevent bending the frame.

### Safety Guidelines
- **UV Rating is Critical:** Only use sunglasses rated for 100% UV protection (UV400). Tinted lenses without UV protection are more dangerous than no sunglasses, as the dark tint causes your pupils to dilate, letting in more harmful UV radiation.
- **Impact Hazards:** Use polycarbonate or Trivex lenses if engaging in physical labor or moving through dense brush, as glass lenses can shatter into the eyes upon impact.
- **Low Light Danger:** Remove sunglasses at dusk, in dense shade, or indoors to prevent tripping hazards and accidents.
- **Common Beginner Mistakes:** Leaving sunglasses face-down on abrasive surfaces, leaving them on a car dashboard in extreme heat (which can warp frames and ruin lens coatings), and wiping them with abrasive fabrics.

### Practice & Skill Building
Practice transitioning your eyewear between varying light conditions (e.g., walking from bright sun into a dark forest canopy) to gauge how quickly your eyes adjust. Get accustomed to moving securely while peripheral vision is slightly obscured by thicker "wrap-around" survival frames. A common mistake is losing sunglasses by placing them on top of the head; build the habit of storing them securely in a hard case or hanging them on a secure lanyard when not over the eyes.

## Quality Assessment

### Quality Indicators
- **Certifications:** Look for markings indicating CE, ANSI Z80.3 (US), or ISO 12312-1 compliance. 
- **UV Labeling:** Explicit "UV400" or "100% UVA/UVB protection" stickers/engravings.
- **Optical Clarity:** High-quality lenses will not distort straight lines when you look through them and move your head side-to-side.
- **Build Quality:** Sturdy hinges (often barrel or spring hinges made of metal), symmetrical frames, and even weight distribution.
- **Polarization (Optional but common):** Can be tested by looking at an LCD screen (like a phone) through the lenses and rotating the glasses 90 degrees; the screen should turn black.

### Durability & Lifespan
- **Expected lifespan:** 2–5 years under heavy, normal use; indefinitely if stored correctly.
- **Factors that affect longevity:** Exposure to extreme heat, saltwater, sunscreen chemicals, DEET insect repellent (melts plastics), and physical drops.
- **Signs of degradation:** Peeling, bubbling, or flaking of the lens coatings (delamination); deep scratches in the line of sight; loose hinges; brittle or sticky plastic frames.

## Maintenance & Repair

### Routine Maintenance
- **Daily:** Rinse with clean water to remove abrasive grit, then gently wipe dry with a clean microfiber cloth.
- **Weekly:** Wash gently with mild dish soap (free of lotions) and lukewarm water to remove skin oils and sweat. 
- **Monthly:** Check hinge screws and tighten them using an optical screwdriver.

### Common Repairs

| Issue | Solution | Difficulty | Required Tools |
|-------|----------|------------|----------------|
| Loose temples | Tighten the hinge screw | Easy | Micro-screwdriver |
| Missing screw | Replace with a spare from a repair kit | Easy | Micro-screwdriver, spare screw |

| Bent wire frame | Gently apply pressure with padded pliers to reshape | Medium | Pliers with cloth padding |

## Storage Guidelines

### Optimal Storage Conditions
- **Temperature:** 10–25°C (50–77°F). Avoid extreme temperatures above 40°C.
- **Humidity:** Moderate (30–50%).
- **Light exposure:** Store in a dark environment when not in use to protect frame polymers.
- **Container:** Always store in a crush-proof hard-shell case lined with microfiber or soft cloth.

### Degradation Prevention
To prevent the most common degradation (lens delamination and frame warping): never leave sunglasses on a car dashboard; always rinse off saltwater or pool water immediately; keep DEET-based bug sprays far away from plastic frames and polycarbonate lenses.

## Health & Safety Considerations

### Short-term Risks
- **Impaired Vision:** Wearing dark lenses in low-light environments increases the risk of trips, falls, and accidents.
- **Physical Discomfort:** Poorly fitting frames can cause pressure headaches behind the ears or on the bridge of the nose.

### Long-term Health Impact

| Risk Factor | Potential Impact | Mitigation Strategy |
|-------------|------------------|---------------------|
| Inadequate UV protection | Cataracts, macular degeneration, photokeratitis (snow blindness) *(Documented)* | Strictly use lenses verified for 100% UVA/UVB (UV400) protection. |
| Poor optical clarity | Chronic eye strain, headaches, nausea *(Documented)* | Use high-quality, distortion-free lenses; avoid cheap novelty glasses. |

## Common Misconceptions

1. **Myth:** Darker lenses provide better UV protection.  
   **Reality:** Lens darkness (tint) only reduces visible light (glare). UV protection comes from an invisible chemical coating. Dark lenses without UV protection are actively harmful.

2. **Myth:** Polarized lenses protect against UV rays.  
   **Reality:** Polarization only filters horizontal light waves to reduce glare off water, snow, and roads. It does not block UV rays unless a specific UV coating is also applied.

3. **Myth:** Minor lens scratches don't matter as long as you can see.  
   **Reality:** Scratches cause light to scatter, leading to eye strain and headaches. Deep scratches can also compromise the UV-blocking layer, letting harmful rays through.

4. **Myth:** Glass lenses are the best choice for survival gear.  
   **Reality:** While glass is highly scratch-resistant, it is heavy and can shatter upon impact, posing a severe risk to the eyes. Polycarbonate is the superior choice for tactical/survival use due to its shatter resistance.

5. **Myth:** You only need sunglasses on sunny summer days.  
   **Reality:** UV rays penetrate cloud cover, and UV exposure is often highest in winter when light reflects off snow (causing snow blindness). They are a year-round necessity.

## DIY / Manufacturing Guide

### Feasibility
**Can be made at home:** Partially (Emergency Slit Goggles)

### Instructions (for Emergency Slit Goggles)
In a wilderness survival situation (especially snowy environments), you can craft traditional Inuit-style snow goggles to prevent snow blindness.

1. Find a piece of wood, bark, bone, or stiff plastic roughly the width of your face.
2. Carve or cut the material so it rests comfortably over the bridge of your nose and against your cheekbones.
3. Cut a very narrow horizontal slit (about 1–2 mm wide) across the center where your eyes align.
4. Poke a small hole in each end of the material.
5. Thread cordage, string, or a strip of cloth through the holes to tie the goggles around your head.
6. (Optional) Rub charcoal or soot on the inside of the goggles to further reduce glare.

**Required Materials:**
- Wood, bark, bone, or opaque plastic
- Cordage or string

**Required Tools:**
- Knife or sharp cutting tool

**Estimated Time:** 1–2 hours

**Safety Notes:** Slit goggles significantly reduce peripheral vision. They do not block 100% of UV rays but work by drastically reducing the total volume of light entering the eye. Use extreme caution when moving while wearing them.

### Why Not DIY (Full Sunglasses)
Manufacturing true optical-grade, shatter-resistant polycarbonate lenses with consistent, distortion-free curves and a stable, chemical 100% UV-blocking coating requires specialized industrial injection-molding equipment and chemical vapor deposition processes. Creating these at home safely is impossible, and attempting to use improvised tinted plastics can permanently damage your eyes by dilating the pupils without blocking UV radiation.

Here is a detailed breakdown of the three primary lens materials and how they perform in survival and emergency preparedness scenarios. 

When choosing eyewear for rugged environments, the primary trade-offs are usually between **impact resistance** (safety), **scratch resistance** (durability), and **optical clarity** (reducing eye strain).

---

## Lens Material Comparison

| Feature | Polycarbonate | Trivex | Glass |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Impact Resistance** | Excellent (Shatterproof) | Excellent (Shatterproof) | Poor (Shatters easily) |
| **Scratch Resistance** | Low (Requires coating) | Medium | Excellent (Highest) |
| **Optical Clarity** | Good (Some distortion) | Very Good | Outstanding (No distortion) |
| **Weight** | Very Light | Lightest | Heavy |
| **Chemical Resistance** | Poor (Melts with DEET) | Excellent | Excellent |
| **Cost** | Low to Moderate | High | Moderate to High |

## 1. Polycarbonate
Polycarbonate was originally developed for aerospace applications and helmet visors. It is the most common material used in tactical, sports, and safety eyewear.

* **The Pros:** It is highly impact-resistant. If struck by a branch, flying debris, or even some low-velocity shrapnel, it will dent or deform rather than shatter into your eyes. It is also inherently 100% UV-blocking without the need for extra coatings, and it is very lightweight.
* **The Cons:** It is a relatively soft material, making it highly susceptible to scratching. Even with factory anti-scratch coatings, wiping it with a gritty shirt will damage it over time. It also has a lower "Abbe value" (a measure of optical clarity), which can cause slight peripheral distortion. Finally, it reacts disastrously to common harsh chemicals—specifically DEET insect repellent, which will literally melt and permanently cloud the plastic.
* **Survival Verdict:** **The Standard Workhorse.** It is the minimum standard for any active survival or tactical kit due to its safety profile, but you must protect it in a hard case and keep it away from DEET.

## 2. Trivex
Trivex is a newer, urethane-based monomer initially developed for military visual armor. 

* **The Pros:** Trivex offers the same shatterproof impact resistance as polycarbonate but solves almost all of its drawbacks. It is about 10% lighter than polycarbonate (making it the lightest lens material available). It has a much higher Abbe value, meaning the optical clarity rivals glass, significantly reducing eye fatigue during long days of observation. Crucially for survivalists, it is highly chemical-resistant—getting DEET, sunscreen, or fuels on Trivex will not ruin the lens.
* **The Cons:** It is noticeably more expensive than polycarbonate and is less common, meaning you often have to seek out premium eyewear brands or custom prescription makers to get it. 
* **Survival Verdict:** **The Premium Choice.** If budget allows, Trivex is the ultimate survival lens material. It provides the safety of polycarbonate with better vision, lighter weight, and immunity to bug spray.

## 3. Glass (Crown Glass)
Glass was the original sunglass material and is still preferred by many high-end optical brands.

* **The Pros:** Nothing beats the optical clarity of pure glass. It provides a crisp, distortion-free view that prevents headaches and eye strain better than any plastic. It is also incredibly scratch-resistant; you can wipe a dusty glass lens with a coarse sleeve without ruining it. It is entirely unaffected by chemicals, heat, and time.
* **The Cons:** Glass is heavy. A heavy frame will constantly slide down your nose when sweating and can cause pressure aches on the bridge of your nose. Most importantly, **glass shatters upon impact**. If you trip and hit your face, or a branch snaps back and strikes your lens, glass can send microscopic shards directly into your eye, turning a minor accident into a blinding medical emergency.
* **Survival Verdict:** **Use with Extreme Caution.** Glass is excellent for static or low-risk activities like driving, fishing on a boat, or casual wear. However, it should be strictly avoided for wilderness traversal, tactical movements, or rugged survival tasks due to the catastrophic risk of shattering.

Choosing the right eyewear in a survival scenario is just as critical as selecting the right footwear. It dictates your ability to navigate safely, spot resources or hazards, and avoid debilitating conditions like snow blindness. 

To understand how sunglasses function in the wild, we have to look at two distinct features: **polarization** (which manages glare) and **lens tint** (which manages contrast and color).

Here is the breakdown of how these features affect your vision across different extreme environments.

---

### The Baseline: How Polarization Works
When sunlight hits a flat, reflective surface (like a lake or a snowfield), the light waves align horizontally, creating an intense, blinding glare. Polarized lenses have a special chemical filter that acts like microscopic vertical blinds, blocking this horizontal light while letting vertical light pass through. 



While polarization is generally fantastic for reducing eye strain, it is not universally ideal for every single survival situation, as we will see below.

---

### Environment 1: Open Water
Out on the water, you are dealing with uninterrupted UV exposure and massive amounts of horizontal glare bouncing off the surface. Your primary goals are to protect your retinas and see beneath the surface to spot fish, shallows, or submerged hazards.

* **Polarization:** **Absolutely essential.** Without it, the surface of the water acts like a mirror, blinding you and hiding everything underneath. 
* **Gray Tint:** Best for **deep, open ocean**. Gray is a "neutral" tint. It drastically reduces overall brightness without distorting colors. It keeps your eyes relaxed during long hours under a harsh, unobstructed sun.
* **Copper/Brown Tint:** Best for **shallow water, flats, and rivers**. Copper blocks blue light, which significantly boosts contrast. This helps your eyes separate the colors of the underwater environment, allowing you to spot the distinct shapes of fish or rocks against sandy or weedy bottoms.

### Environment 2: Dense Forests and Jungles
Under a heavy canopy, your lighting conditions are incredibly variable. You are dealing with low overall light, dappled shadows, and the need to quickly identify trip hazards, roots, and wildlife hiding in the brush.

* **Yellow or Amber Tint:** **The clear winner.** Yellow tints excel in low-light and overcast conditions. They filter out blue light, which immediately sharpens focus, enhances depth perception, and makes your surroundings look brighter and more defined. 
* **Gray/Copper Tint:** Generally a poor choice here. Darker tints will make a shaded forest dangerously dark, obscuring details and causing eye strain as your pupils struggle to dilate.
* **Polarization:** **Optional, sometimes detrimental.** While polarization reduces glare off wet leaves, it can also hide the reflective sheen of wet, slippery rocks or roots. In a dense forest, spotting that moisture is a crucial navigational cue to avoid a sprained ankle.

### Environment 3: Snow and Alpine Landscapes
Snow reflects up to 80% of UV radiation, meaning your eyes are getting hit from the sun above and the ground below. The primary threats are "snow blindness" (sunburn of the cornea) and "flat light" (when an overcast sky eliminates shadows, making it impossible to see the contours of drop-offs or crevasses).

* **Polarization:** **Highly recommended for bright days.** It cuts the blinding glare bouncing off the snowpack. *Note:* Some mountaineers and ice climbers prefer non-polarized lenses because polarization can mask the glare of black ice, making it harder to spot slick patches.
* **Gray Tint:** Best for **bright, bluebird days**. A very dark gray lens will filter out the maximum amount of harsh light, keeping your eyes comfortable and protected. 
* **Rose, Copper, or Yellow Tint:** Best for **overcast, snowy days**. When the clouds roll in and the snowfield turns into a featureless white void (flat light), you need a contrast-enhancing tint like rose or copper. These tints force shadows to stand out, allowing you to read the terrain and spot sudden dips or snowdrifts.
