# Axe
## Overview
An axe is a fundamental tool consisting of a head, typically made of metal, attached to a handle, or haft, usually made of wood. In survival and emergency preparedness, it is indispensable for felling trees, chopping and splitting wood for fire and shelter construction, and can also be used for clearing paths, self-defense, and as a general-purpose cutting and hammering tool. Its versatility makes it a cornerstone of any preparedness kit.

---
## Usage & Safety Protocols
### How to Use
1. **Inspect the Axe:** Before each use, check that the head is tight and the handle is free of cracks or splinters.
2. **Clear Your Workspace:** Ensure you have a clear, flat area to work in, free from tripping hazards or overhead branches. Maintain a safe distance from bystanders, typically at least 16 feet.
3. **Assume a Proper Stance:** Stand with your feet shoulder-width apart, with one foot slightly forward for balance. This provides a stable base for swinging.
4. **Grip Securely:** Hold the handle with both hands. Your dominant hand should be closer to the head, and your non-dominant hand near the bottom of the handle for maximum control and power.
5. **Swing with Control:** Use your legs and core to generate power, not just your arms. Focus on accuracy over raw strength, allowing your body weight to drive the swing. Always direct the axe head away from your body.

### Safety Guidelines
- **Critical safety warnings:** Never use a dull axe, as it is more likely to glance off wood and cause injury. Never throw an axe unless in a designated and safe axe-throwing environment. A loose head can detach mid-swing and become a dangerous projectile.
- **Proper handling procedures:** When carrying an axe, always use a sheath to cover the blade. Hold it by the handle with the head pointing down and away from your body. When passing an axe, ensure the other person has a firm grip before you let go.
- **Emergency protocols:** In case of injury, apply direct pressure to the wound and seek immediate medical attention. Always have a first-aid kit readily available.
- **Common beginner mistakes to avoid during use:** Avoid swinging with only your arms, which leads to fatigue and loss of control. Don't chop on unstable ground or near obstacles. A common mistake is using an incorrect blade angle, which can cause the axe to bounce back.

### Practice & Skill Building
To practice effectively, start with smaller, softer wood and focus on technique rather than power. A common beginner mistake is trying to chop through a log in one swing. Instead, aim for a "V" shaped notch, alternating your swings from each side. This is more efficient and safer. Another mistake is poor aim; practice by drawing a target on the log to improve accuracy.

## Quality Assessment
### Quality Indicators
- **Head:** A high-quality axe head is forged from high-carbon steel, which holds a sharp edge. Look for a smooth finish without cracks or pitting. A "ring test" (tapping the head and listening for a clear, ringing sound) can indicate good quality metal without internal flaws.[6254]
- **Handle:** The best handles are made from straight-grained hickory or ash, as these woods are both strong and absorb shock well. The grain should run parallel to the length of the handle. Avoid handles with prominent or irregular grain patterns, as these are weak points.[6254]
- **Fit:** The head should be tightly fitted to the handle with no gaps or movement. A wooden wedge and sometimes a metal cross-wedge are used to secure it.

### Durability & Lifespan
- **Expected lifespan under normal use:** A well-maintained, high-quality axe can last a lifetime, or even generations. Handles are the most common point of failure and may need replacement every 5-10 years with heavy use.
- **Factors that affect longevity:** Exposure to moisture is the primary factor, leading to rust on the head and rot in the handle. Improper use, such as striking the back of the axe head or using it as a pry bar, can damage the eye and loosen the handle.
- **Signs of degradation or failure to watch for:** Look for rust on the head, cracks or splinters in the handle, and any looseness between the head and handle.

## Maintenance & Repair
### Routine Maintenance
Clean the axe head after each use to remove sap and moisture. Apply a light coat of oil to the head to prevent rust. The wooden handle should be periodically treated with boiled linseed oil or wax to keep it from drying out. Always keep the blade sharp using a file or sharpening stone.

### Common Repairs
| Issue | Solution | Difficulty | Required Tools |
|---|---|---|---|
| Loose Axe Head | Remove old wedges and insert a new, slightly larger wooden wedge, followed by a metal cross-wedge if needed. Soaking the new wedge can help it swell for a tighter fit.  | Easy | Hammer, chisel, new wedges |
| Dull Blade | Use a mill file to restore the edge profile, followed by a sharpening puck or stone to hone the edge. Maintain a consistent angle and file in one direction.  | Medium | Mill file, sharpening stone/puck, gloves, vise |
| Cracked Handle | For minor cracks (<25% of the handle), epoxy and clamps can be used. For major cracks, the handle must be replaced.  | Medium | Epoxy, clamps |
| Broken Handle | Saw off the old handle just below the axe head. Drill out the remaining wood from the eye. Fit a new handle by rasping it to shape, then secure with wedges.  | Hard | Saw, drill, rasp/file, hammer, wedges |

### Required Consumables & Tools
- **Maintenance supplies:** Boiled linseed oil or wax, rust-prevention oil, cleaning cloths.
- **Repair tools:** Mill file, sharpening stone/puck, hammer, wedges, rasp, saw, drill.
- **Replacement parts:** Replacement handles (hickory or ash), wooden and metal wedges.

## Storage Guidelines
### Optimal Storage Conditions
- **Temperature range:** Store in a cool, stable environment. Avoid extreme temperature fluctuations that can cause wood to expand and contract.
- **Humidity requirements:** Store in a dry, well-ventilated area with 50-60% humidity to prevent rust on the head and drying of the handle.
- **Light exposure:** Avoid direct sunlight.
- **Container or packaging recommendations:** Always store with a protective sheath or cover over the blade. It can be hung on a wall rack or stored in a dedicated tool cabinet.

### Degradation Prevention
To prevent rust, keep the axe head clean, dry, and lightly oiled. To prevent the handle from drying and cracking, treat it regularly with boiled linseed oil. Store the axe vertically or hanging to prevent damage to the blade and handle.

## Health & Safety Considerations
### Short-term Risks
The most significant short-term risk is severe laceration from accidental contact with the blade. Flying wood chips can cause eye injuries. Improper swinging technique or a poorly maintained axe can lead to the head detaching or glancing off wood, causing unpredictable and dangerous ricochets.

### Long-term Health Impact
| Risk Factor | Potential Impact | Mitigation Strategy |
|---|---|---|
| Repetitive Strain | *Documented:* Chronic joint pain, tendonitis, or muscle strain from repeated, forceful swinging. | Use proper technique that engages the whole body, not just the arms. Take regular breaks. |
| Vibration | *Documented:* Hand-Arm Vibration Syndrome (HAVS), leading to nerve damage, numbness, and poor circulation in the hands and fingers. | Use axes with well-designed handles that absorb shock (e.g., hickory). Wear anti-vibration gloves. |
| Material Toxicity | *Theoretical:* Older axes may have coatings or treatments of unknown composition. The primary risk is from dust inhalation during sanding/repair. | Wear a dust mask and work in a well-ventilated area when repairing or modifying old axes. Modern axes from reputable brands are generally safe. |

## Common Misconceptions
6. **Myth:** You need to be very strong to use an axe effectively.
    **Reality:** Technique is far more important than strength. A proper swing utilizes body weight and momentum, allowing a person of average strength to chop wood efficiently and safely. 
7. **Myth:** A heavier axe is always better for splitting wood.
    **Reality:** While a heavy splitting maul is effective for large, knotty rounds, an axe that is too heavy for the user can lead to rapid fatigue and loss of control. The right weight depends on the user's strength and the task. 
8. **Myth:** Duct tape is a good temporary fix for a loose axe handle.
    **Reality:** Duct tape provides a false sense of security. It cannot secure a heavy axe head under the force of a swing. The only safe fix is to properly re-seat the head with wedges. 
9. **Myth:** You should never use a "dull" axe because it's safer.
    **Reality:** A dull axe is far more dangerous than a sharp one. It requires more force to cut, is more likely to glance off the wood, and can cause a serious accident. A sharp axe bites into the wood predictably. 
10. **Myth:** Any type of wood will work for a replacement handle.
    **Reality:** Handle wood must be both strong and shock-absorbent. Hickory and ash are the preferred choices due to their grain structure and flexibility. Using improper wood can lead to handle failure during use. 
11. **Myth:** All axes are basically the same.[6254]
    **Reality:** Axes are highly specialized. Felling axes have thin, sharp bits for cutting across the grain, while splitting axes have a wider, wedge-shaped head for splitting wood along the grain. Using the wrong type of axe for the job is inefficient and can be dangerous.[6802]

## DIY / Manufacturing Guide
### Feasibility
**Can be made at home:** Partially

### Instructions (if feasible)
A complete axe is difficult to make at home without specialized blacksmithing equipment. However, replacing a handle is a common and feasible DIY project.

12. **Remove the old handle:** Secure the axe head in a vise and saw off the handle just below the head. Use a drill and a punch to remove the remaining wood from the eye.
13. **Select a new handle:** Choose a pre-made hickory or ash handle, or shape your own from a blank. Ensure the grain is straight and oriented correctly.
14. **Fit the handle:** Carefully shave or file down the top of the handle (the tenon) until it fits snugly into the axe head's eye. There should be no gaps.
15. **Secure the handle:** Drive the handle into the eye. Insert a wooden wedge into the kerf (slit) at the top of the handle and hammer it in firmly. Then, drive a metal wedge in perpendicularly to the wooden one for extra security.
16. **Finish:** Saw off any excess wood from the top of the eye and seal it with boiled linseed oil.

**Required Materials:**
- Replacement axe handle (hickory or ash)
- Wooden and metal wedges
- Boiled linseed oil

**Required Tools:**
- Vise
- Saw
- Drill
- Hammer
- Rasp or file

**Estimated Time:** 1-3 hours

**Safety Notes:** Always wear safety glasses and gloves. When removing the old handle, be careful not to damage the axe head eye. Ensure the new handle is securely fitted before use.

### Why Not DIY (if not feasible)
Forging an axe head from raw steel is not feasible for home DIY without extensive training and equipment, including a forge, anvil, power hammer, and various specialized tools. The process requires precise temperature control and metallurgical knowledge to ensure the steel is properly hardened and tempered. An improperly made head can be brittle and shatter dangerously during use.
