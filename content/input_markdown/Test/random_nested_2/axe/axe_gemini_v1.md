# Axe

## Overview

An axe is an essential hand tool featuring a heavy steel blade attached to a wooden or composite handle, designed for chopping, splitting, shaping, and piercing wood. In survival and emergency preparedness scenarios, axes are indispensable for processing firewood, constructing durable shelters, clearing debris, and, if necessary, serving as a formidable tool for self-defense.

---

## Usage & Safety Protocols

### How to Use

1. **Clear the Area:** Establish a "blood circle" (swing radius plus the length of the axe and your arm) and ensure no people, pets, or overhead obstructions are within it.
    
2. **Inspect the Tool:** Check that the axe head is firmly attached to the handle and the edge is appropriately sharp.
    
3. **Assume the Stance:** Stand with your feet shoulder-width apart, perpendicular to the target. Use a chopping block rather than chopping directly on the ground.
    
4. **Grip the Handle:** Place your non-dominant hand at the base of the handle. Place your dominant hand near the axe head (the "throat").
    
5. **Execute the Swing:** Raise the axe over your shoulder. As you swing down, slide your dominant hand down the handle to meet your non-dominant hand at the base, utilizing gravity and your core, rather than just arm strength.
    
6. **Follow Through:** Allow the axe's momentum to carry it through the wood, maintaining a firm grip. Ensure the axe's trajectory would pass between your legs or safely to the side if it misses or deflects.
    

### Safety Guidelines

- **Critical safety warnings:** Never chop toward your own body, legs, or feet. Always use a stable chopping block.
    
- **Proper handling procedures:** Carry the axe by the handle just below the head, with the blade facing downward and away from your body. Always use a sheath when not in use.
    
- **Emergency protocols:** Keep a comprehensive first-aid kit with tourniquets and pressure dressings nearby. In case of a deep laceration, apply direct pressure immediately and seek emergency medical attention.
    
- **Common beginner mistakes to avoid during use:** "Overstriking" (hitting the handle against the wood instead of the blade), swinging with too much force, and taking eyes off the target during the swing.
    

### Practice & Skill Building

To practice effectively, start with a small hatchet and smaller pieces of soft wood before graduating to a full-sized felling or splitting axe. Focus entirely on accuracy and form rather than power; let the weight of the head do the work. A common beginner mistake is muscling the swing, which leads to early fatigue and dangerous deflections. Avoid this by practicing a slow, controlled slide of the upper hand down the handle.

## Quality Assessment

### Quality Indicators

A high-quality axe will feature a forged high-carbon steel head that rings clearly when tapped. For wooden handles, look for straight grain orientation that runs parallel to the axe blade (this prevents snapping), made from dense hardwoods like American Hickory or Ash. The head should be immovably secured to the handle, typically with a wooden wedge and a metal cross-wedge. The edge should be symmetrically ground without burrs.

### Durability & Lifespan

- **Expected lifespan under normal use:** 20 to 50+ years (practically indefinite with proper maintenance and occasional handle replacement).
    
- **Factors that affect longevity:** Exposure to moisture, poor swing technique (overstrikes), improper storage, and neglecting protective oiling.
    
- **Signs of degradation or failure to watch for:** A loose or rattling head, cracks or splinters anywhere on the handle, deep rust pitting on the steel, or severe chips/rolls on the cutting edge.

## Maintenance & Repair

### Routine Maintenance

- **Wiping Down:** After every use, wipe away dirt and moisture.
    
- **Oiling:** Lightly coat the steel head with oil (gun oil, 3-in-1, or camellia oil) after use. Wipe the wooden handle with boiled linseed oil (BLO) every 3–6 months.
    
- **Sharpening:** Touch up the edge with a sharpening puck or bastard file every few uses, or immediately if nicked.

## Storage Guidelines

### Optimal Storage Conditions

- **Temperature range:** 10°C to 25°C (50°F to 77°F). Avoid extreme fluctuations.
    
- **Humidity requirements:** 40%–50%. Too dry, and the wood shrinks (causing a loose head). Too humid, and the steel rusts.
    
- **Light exposure:** Store in a dark or shaded place; prolonged UV exposure degrades wood fibers over time.
    
- **Container or packaging recommendations:** Always store with a leather or Kydex sheath protecting the blade. Hang the axe horizontally on a wall or store it vertically with the head resting on a dry, wooden shelf (never on concrete).
    

### Degradation Prevention

To prevent the most common degradation (rust and handle rot/shrinkage), never store the axe outdoors, in a damp basement, or directly near a wood stove or heater. Apply a thick layer of oil to the head and handle before long-term storage, and check the tightness of the head periodically.

Severe lacerations, amputations, crushed appendages (from dropped axe heads), eye injuries from flying wood splinters or broken steel, and acute muscle/tendon strain from improper swinging mechanics.

## Common Misconceptions

1. **Myth:** A sharper axe is always a better axe.
    
    **Reality:** Axe edge geometry depends on its purpose. A splitting axe needs a thicker, wedge-like profile to force wood fibers apart. If it has a razor-thin edge, it will bite into the wood and get hopelessly stuck, or the edge will chip.
    
2. **Myth:** You can fix a loose axe head permanently by soaking it in water.
    
    **Reality:** Water swells the wood temporarily, making the head feel tight. However, as the water evaporates, the wood shrinks back even smaller than before, making the head looser and damaging the wood fibers permanently. Use boiled linseed oil instead.
    
3. **Myth:** Chopping power comes primarily from your arm strength.
    
    **Reality:** Maximum power and efficiency come from your core, legs, and gravity. You should drop your weight into the swing rather than forcing it with your arms.
    
4. **Myth:** One axe is suitable for all wood-processing tasks.
    
    **Reality:** Felling axes are designed to cut across the grain. Splitting mauls are designed to burst along the grain. Using a felling axe for heavy splitting is highly inefficient and risks breaking the handle.
    
5. **Myth:** An axe head should be rigidly blocked if it gets stuck in a log.
    
    **Reality:** Wiggling a stuck axe side-to-side severely damages the handle and can snap it. You should rock it up and down (parallel to the blade) or strike the wood against the chopping block to free it.

**Can be made at home:** Partially (hanging/assembling an axe and carving a handle is highly feasible; forging the steel head is not feasible for most without a blacksmithing setup).

6. **Select the Wood:** Source a piece of straight-grained, dried hardwood (Hickory, Ash, or Oak).
    
7. **Trace and Rough Shape:** Trace the pattern of an axe handle onto the wood. Use a drawknife, band saw, or hatchet to rough out the shape.
    
8. **Refine the Shape:** Use wood rasps and sandpaper to refine the grip, ensuring it feels ergonomic and comfortable in your hands.
    
9. **Shape the Eye (Tenon):** Carefully rasp the top of the handle so it precisely fits the "eye" (the hole) of your steel axe head. It should be a friction fit.
    
10. **Cut the Kerf:** Cut a vertical slit (kerf) into the top of the handle, going down about two-thirds the depth of the axe head.
    
11. **Seat the Head:** Drive the handle firmly into the axe head using a wooden mallet.
    
12. **Wedge It:** Apply wood glue to a wooden wedge and drive it deeply into the kerf to spread the wood tight against the steel eye. Cut off the excess wedge protruding from the top.
    
13. **Add Metal Cross-Wedge:** Drive a metal step-wedge diagonally across the wooden wedge for maximum security.
    
14. **Finish:** Sand smooth and apply multiple coats of boiled linseed oil.

### Why Not DIY (if not feasible)

Forging the actual high-carbon steel axe head at home requires an anvil, a forge capable of reaching over 1000°C (1832°F), proper tongs, heavy hammers, and a deep understanding of metallurgy, heat treating, and tempering. Incorrectly tempered steel will either be too brittle (shattering like glass upon impact) or too soft (rolling and failing to hold an edge).

Here is a step-by-step guide to safely and effectively sharpening your axe using a hand file and a sharpening puck.

This method will restore a damaged edge and bring it back to a working sharpness suitable for chopping and splitting wood.

---

### Tools Required

- **Mill Bastard File:** A 10-inch or 12-inch single-cut file for reshaping and removing chips.
    
- **Dual-Grit Sharpening Puck:** A round whetstone with a coarse side and a fine side.
    
- **Lubricant:** Honing oil, 3-in-1 oil, or water (for the puck).
    
- **Heavy Leather Gloves:** Essential for protecting your hands from the blade and metal filings.
    
- **Sturdy Vise or Clamps:** To secure the axe head safely.
    
- **Clean Rag & Rust-Preventative Oil:** For cleaning and protecting the blade afterward.
    

---

### Step 1: Preparation and Securing the Axe

Safety is the absolute priority when sharpening. Never try to hold the axe between your knees or freehand it while filing.

Lock the axe head firmly in a bench vise. Position it so the cutting edge is facing upward and horizontal to the floor. If you do not have a vise, use heavy-duty C-clamps to secure the axe head to the edge of a sturdy workbench. Put on your heavy leather gloves to protect your hands from slipping against the edge.

### Step 2: Edge Assessment

Inspect the cutting edge under good lighting. Look for shiny spots (which indicate dull, flattened areas), rolls, or visible chips in the steel.

Take note of the axe's natural bevel (the angle of the edge). For a felling axe, this is typically a thinner, slightly convex curve. For a splitting axe or maul, the angle will be much blunter and steeper. Your goal is to follow the manufacturer's original angle, not invent a new one.

### Step 3: Profiling with the Hand File

The file is used to remove metal quickly, erase chips, and establish the primary bevel.

Grip the file handle with your dominant hand and pinch the tip of the file with your non-dominant hand. Lay the file flat against the existing bevel.

Apply moderate downward pressure and push the file forward across the blade, sliding it slightly from the heel (bottom) to the toe (top) of the axe edge in a sweeping motion.

Lift the file completely off the steel on the return stroke. Files only cut on the push stroke; dragging it backward will ruin the file's teeth. Count your strokes and repeat this process until the chips are gone and you feel a slight burr (a rough wire edge) form on the opposite side of the blade.

Unclamp the axe, flip it over, re-clamp it, and perform the exact same number of strokes on the other side until the bevels meet evenly in the middle.

### Step 4: Honing with the Coarse Puck

Once the edge is properly shaped with the file, it is time to refine it.

Apply a few drops of honing oil or water to the coarse side of your sharpening puck. Grip the puck firmly by the edges, ensuring your fingers do not hang over the bottom where they could touch the axe blade.

Place the coarse side of the puck against the bevel, matching the angle you just filed. Use tight, overlapping circular motions to work your way up and down the edge of the blade. Maintain consistent pressure. Count your passes and do this evenly on both sides of the axe head until the deep scratches left by the file are smoothed out.

### Step 5: Polishing with the Fine Puck

Flip the puck over to the fine-grit side. Apply a little more oil or water.

Repeat the exact same tight, overlapping circular motions along the edge of the blade. This step removes the scratches left by the coarse grit and hones the edge to a sharp, finished state.

Alternate sides frequently during this final stage (e.g., work one side for a minute, then flip and do the other). Continue until the edge is smooth, unified, and sharp to the touch.

### Step 6: Clean Up and Protection

Wipe the axe head entirely clean with a rag to remove all metal filings, grit, and dirty oil.

Apply a fresh, thin coat of rust-preventative oil (like gun oil, camellia oil, or 3-in-1 oil) to the bare steel. This prevents environmental moisture from rusting your newly exposed edge. Carefully place the axe back in its protective leather or Kydex sheath.
