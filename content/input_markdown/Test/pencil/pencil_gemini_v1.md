# Pencil

## Overview

A pencil is a hand-held writing or drawing instrument consisting of a solid pigment core (typically graphite mixed with clay) encased in a protective cylinder, usually made of wood. In survival and emergency preparedness scenarios, pencils are highly valued because they do not rely on ink that can dry out or freeze, they can write in upside-down or wet conditions, and their wood shavings serve as excellent emergency fire tinder.

---

## Usage & Safety Protocols

### How to Use

1. Obtain a sharpener or a survival knife to expose the inner graphite core.
    
2. Carefully shave away the wooden casing at one end until approximately 0.5 to 1 cm of the core is exposed and shaped into a point.
    
3. Grip the pencil firmly between your thumb and index finger, resting it on your middle finger.
    
4. Apply light to moderate pressure against a writing surface (paper, wood, stone) to leave a mark.
    
5. Resharpen when the tip becomes too blunt to write legibly.
    

### Safety Guidelines

- **Critical safety warnings:** The sharpened point can act as a puncture hazard, especially if carried loose in a pocket.
    
- **Proper handling procedures:** Carry in a protective case or point-down in a secure pouch. Do not run or walk quickly while holding a sharpened pencil pointing toward your body or face.
    
- **Emergency protocols:** If an accidental puncture wound occurs, wash the area thoroughly with soap and water to prevent infection. Contrary to popular belief, there is no risk of lead poisoning from a pencil puncture, as the core is graphite. Remove any visible graphite or wood splinters with sterilized tweezers.
    
- **Common beginner mistakes to avoid during use:** Pressing too hard (which snaps the tip), over-sharpening (which wastes the tool), and chewing on the wooden end (which can cause splintering).
    

### Practice & Skill Building

To maximize a pencil's utility in a survival context, practice sharpening it with a standard pocket knife rather than a dedicated sharpener. Beginners commonly carve too deeply, breaking the core or whittling away too much wood. Practice making long, shallow, controlled shaving strokes away from your body. Additionally, practice writing on alternative surfaces like smooth bark, dried leaves, or damp paper to understand the pressure required in non-ideal conditions.

## Quality Assessment

### Quality Indicators

- **Materials:** High-quality pencils use straight-grained woods like incense-cedar, which sharpen smoothly without splintering.
    
- **Markings:** Clearly stamped grading scales (e.g., HB, 2B, 2H) indicating graphite hardness.
    
- **Construction:** The graphite core should be perfectly centered within the wood casing. Off-center cores will break constantly during sharpening.
    
- **Ferrule:** If an eraser is attached, the metal band (ferrule) should be tightly crimped to the wood without rattling.
    

### Durability & Lifespan

- **Expected lifespan under normal use:** A standard 19 cm pencil can draw a line approximately 56 kilometers (35 miles) long or write around 45,000 words. For moderate daily writing, one pencil lasts 3 to 6 weeks.
    
- **Factors that affect longevity:** Excessive moisture, frequent dropping, over-sharpening, and writing pressure.
    
- **Signs of degradation or failure to watch for:** Warped or bowed wooden casings, micro-fractures in the graphite core (causing the tip to repeatedly fall out when sharpened), and hardened/brittle erasers that smear rather than erase.

## Storage Guidelines

### Optimal Storage Conditions

- **Temperature range:** -50°C to 50°C (-58°F to 122°F). Pencils are highly resilient to temperature extremes.
    
- **Humidity requirements:** Low to moderate (20% to 50%). Prolonged high humidity can cause the wood to warp or rot and the glue binding the wood halves to fail.
    
- **Light exposure:** N/A (Light does not significantly degrade wood or graphite).
    
- **Container or packaging recommendations:** Hard-sided crush-proof container (like a plastic tube, tin, or wooden box) to prevent snapping the wood or fracturing the internal graphite core during transit.
    

### Degradation Prevention

Keep pencils strictly away from standing water or prolonged heavy moisture. If pencils get wet, lay them out to dry slowly at room temperature; baking them near a fire will cause the wood to warp or split. Keep unsharpened until needed to protect the graphite core from premature breakage.

## Common Misconceptions

6. **Myth:** Pencils contain lead and can cause lead poisoning if you are stabbed by one.
    
    **Reality:** Pencils have never contained lead. The core is a mixture of graphite (carbon) and clay. A puncture wound leaves a dark mark akin to a small tattoo, but poses no risk of lead poisoning.
    
7. **Myth:** NASA spent millions developing a pen for space, while the Russians just used pencils.
    
    **Reality:** Both NASA and the Soviet Union initially used pencils in space. However, broken pencil tips and flammable graphite dust floating in microgravity posed severe risks to electrical equipment and air filtration. NASA subsequently adopted the pressurized Fisher Space Pen.
    
8. **Myth:** Pencils cannot write on wet paper.
    
    **Reality:** While extreme water can destroy the paper itself, a standard graphite pencil will write quite well on damp or wet paper, unlike standard ballpoint or gel pens which require dry friction to roll ink.
    
9. **Myth:** Pencils have an expiration date.
    
    **Reality:** The wood and graphite components of a pencil can last for centuries. Only the attached rubber eraser degrades (dries out and hardens) over a span of 3 to 10 years.
    
10. **Myth:** Any wood is suitable for making a pencil.
    
    **Reality:** While you can force graphite into many woods, woods with complex or knotty grains will splinter violently when sharpened. Straight-grained cedar or basswood is required for reliable, smooth sharpening.

### Instructions (if feasible)

_Creating a modern, perfectly centered wooden pencil at home is extremely difficult without industrial machinery, but creating a crude survival writing implement is feasible._

11. Source graphite powder (or crush charcoal from a fire into a fine dust).
    
12. Mix the graphite/charcoal dust with a small amount of fine clay (Kaolin) and water to create a thick paste. The more clay you use, the harder the final "lead" will be.

14. Bake the sticks in a kiln or hot fire until the clay hardens and cures.

16. Place the baked graphite stick into the groove, apply wood glue to the flat surfaces, and clamp the two slats together.
    
17. Once dried, whittle or carve the resulting block down into a manageable cylindrical or hexagonal shape.

- Water

- Clamps
    
- Heat source (fire/kiln)

Finding and refining pure Kaolin (often called "china clay") in the wild requires a bit of geological scavenger hunting. In a true survival or primitive crafting scenario, you don't "make" clay from scratch; instead, you harvest clay-rich soil and mechanically refine it to extract the microscopic clay particles.

While pure, white Kaolin is ideal for commercial pencil leads due to its fine particle size and firing properties, **almost any highly refined wild clay** can work for a primitive DIY pencil core.

Here is a comprehensive guide to identifying, finding, and refining fine clay in the wild.

---

## 1. Where to Find Kaolin and Wild Clays

Kaolin is a "primary clay," meaning it is often found right where it originally formed from weathering rocks (usually feldspar-rich rocks like granite). Other wild clays ("secondary clays") have been moved by water and are much more common.

- **Stream Banks and Riverbeds:** Water cuts through the earth, exposing distinct layers of soil. Look for dense, uniform bands of material near the water level or just above it.
    
- **Exposed Roots and Uprooted Trees:** Check the root balls of fallen trees. Trees often pull up clay from the subsoil.
    
- **Road Cuts and Cliff Sides:** Man-made or natural vertical cuts in the earth are excellent cross-sections for spotting clay veins.
    
- **Granite Outcroppings (Specifically for Kaolin):** Pure Kaolin is often found near highly weathered, crumbling granite deposits.
    

---

## 2. How to Identify Fine Clay

You need to distinguish clay from silt, sand, and regular loam. Use your senses to test the material:

### Visual Indicators

- **Color:** Pure Kaolin is brilliant white or pale gray. However, wild clays can be red, orange, brown, or bluish-gray (due to iron and organic impurities). For a primitive pencil, the color doesn't matter much.
    
- **Cracking:** Look for puddles that have dried up. If the dried mud has deep, hexagonal, "alligator skin" cracks and curls up at the edges, it has high clay content.
    

### Tactile Tests (The Best Method)

- **The Smudge Test:** Rub a pinch of the damp soil between your thumb and index finger. Sand feels gritty. Silt feels smooth but powdery (like flour). Clay feels slippery, greasy, or sticky.
    
- **The Ribbon Test:** Take a handful of damp soil and roll it into a ball. Squeeze the ball upward between your thumb and index finger to form a "ribbon."
    
    - If it breaks immediately: Mostly sand/dirt.
        
    - If it forms a ribbon 1–2 inches long before breaking: Good clay content.
        
    - If it forms a ribbon over 2 inches long: Excellent, highly plastic clay.
        
- **The Shine Test:** Scrape a smooth rock or your fingernail across a damp lump of the material. If it leaves a slick, glossy smear, it is rich in clay.
    

---

## 3. How to Refine Wild Soil into Fine Clay

Unless you strike a vein of pure, commercial-grade clay, wild soil contains sand, rocks, and organic matter that will ruin a pencil core. You must extract the microscopic clay particles using a water-based process called **levigation** (or slaking).

### Required Materials:

- Two large containers (buckets, hollowed-out logs, or large pots)
    
- Water
    
- A cloth (shirt, bandana, or tightly woven fabric)
    

### Step-by-Step Levigation Process:

1. **Harvest and Dry:** Dig up your clay-rich soil. Break it into small chunks and let it dry completely in the sun. Dry dirt absorbs water much faster and breaks apart easier than wet mud.
    
2. **Crush:** Once bone-dry, crush the chunks into a fine powder using a rock or crude mortar and pestle.
    
3. **Slake (Mix with Water):** Fill your first container 1/3 full of your crushed dirt. Fill the rest of the container with water. Stir vigorously for several minutes until it looks like chocolate milk. Break up any remaining clumps.
    
4. **The First Settle (Removing Sand/Rocks):** Stop stirring. Wait exactly **2 to 3 minutes**. The heavy sand, pebbles, and coarse silt will rapidly sink to the bottom.
    
5. **Pour Off the Suspension:** Carefully pour the muddy water (which holds the microscopic, floating clay particles) into your second container. Stop pouring before you dump the heavy sludge at the bottom of the first bucket. Discard that sludge.
    
6. **The Second Settle (Harvesting the Clay):** Let the second container sit undisturbed for **24 to 48 hours**. The fine clay particles will slowly drift to the bottom, leaving clear water on top.
    
7. **Decant:** Carefully pour off or siphon out the clear water on top, being careful not to disturb the thick "clay slip" (watery clay pudding) at the bottom.
    
8. **Filter and Dry:** Pour the wet clay slip into your cloth. Tie the cloth into a sack and hang it from a branch. The excess water will drip out over a day or two.
    
9. **Wedge:** Once the clay is the consistency of putty or dough, remove it from the cloth. Knead it thoroughly to remove air bubbles. You now have highly refined, pure clay ready to be mixed with your powdered graphite or charcoal.

## 4. Alternative for Pencils: Ash as a Binder

If you are in an environment where clay is entirely absent (e.g., deep sand deserts or solid bedrock), you can attempt to use finely sifted **hardwood ash** mixed with a tiny amount of tree sap or animal hide glue as a rudimentary binder for your crushed charcoal/graphite, though this will be much more brittle than a clay-based core.
