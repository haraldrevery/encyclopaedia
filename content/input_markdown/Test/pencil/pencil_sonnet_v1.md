# Pencils

## Overview

A pencil is a hand-held writing and drawing instrument consisting of a solid graphite (or colored pigment) core encased in a wooden, plastic, or mechanical barrel. Pencils are among the most reliable tools for emergency preparedness and survival scenarios due to their ability to write in wet conditions, at extreme temperatures, on irregular surfaces, and without any power source or ink supply. Unlike pens, they do not freeze, dry out, or leak, making them a practical all-weather communication tool.

---

## Usage & Safety Protocols

### How to Use

1. **Grip the pencil** approximately 2–3 cm (1 inch) above the tip, between the thumb and index finger, resting on the middle finger. Maintain a relaxed grip to reduce hand fatigue.
2. **Sharpen the tip** before use. A sharp tip produces cleaner lines and requires less pressure. A dull tip increases the risk of breakage and poor legibility.
3. **Hold the pencil at a 45–60° angle** to the writing surface for general writing. A more upright angle (closer to 90°) produces finer, more precise lines; a shallower angle produces broader, softer strokes.
4. **Apply light, consistent pressure.** Pressing too hard breaks the core, damages the paper, and causes grooves that are visible on the page beneath.
5. **Write or draw in the direction that feels most natural.** For most right-handed users this means pulling strokes toward yourself; for left-handed users, adjustments to grip angle help avoid smearing.
6. **Rotate the pencil slightly** as you write to maintain an even tip and extend the life of each sharpening.
7. **Erase mistakes** by applying firm but gentle strokes with an eraser, moving in one direction rather than scrubbing back and forth.
8. **Re-sharpen** when lines become inconsistent, wide, or require noticeably more pressure to produce.

### Safety Guidelines

- **Never place a pencil in your mouth or chew on it.** The wood, paint, and lacquer coatings may contain trace chemicals not intended for ingestion.
- **Keep pencils away from young children (under 3 years)** who may choke on broken tip fragments or small pencil caps.
- **Avoid pointing sharpened pencils toward yourself or others.** Even a moderate-grade pencil tip can puncture skin.
- **Do not store loose, unprotected pencils in bags or pockets** where the tip can break, graphite dust can accumulate, or the point can cause accidental punctures.
- **In survival scenarios, avoid using pencil dust or graphite as a lubricant on skin** without confirming the pencil contains only graphite (some colored or specialty pencils contain chemical pigments).
- **Beginner mistakes to avoid:**
  - Using excessive grip pressure, which causes rapid tip breakage and hand fatigue.
  - Sharpening too aggressively, leaving too long an exposed core that snaps easily.
  - Storing pencils loosely in a pack where tips are damaged.
  - Assuming all pencils write identically — hardness grades vary substantially.

### Practice & Skill Building

- **Practice writing at varying pressures** on different surfaces (wet paper, cardboard, rock, bark) to understand the pencil's limits.
- **Practice emergency notation:** short, clear messages written quickly under stress. Legibility degrades under duress — train for this.
- **Practice sharpening** with both a dedicated sharpener and a knife (fieldcraft skill), as mechanical sharpeners may not be available.
- **Common beginner mistakes:**
  - Gripping too tightly, leading to cramping during extended use.
  - Sharpening to a very long point that breaks immediately on first use.
  - Writing on wet or highly textured surfaces without adjusting angle and pressure.
  - Neglecting to protect pencil tips during transport.

## Quality Assessment

### Quality Indicators

- **Core consistency:** A quality pencil produces a smooth, unbroken line without skipping or scratching. Gritty or scratchy performance indicates poor graphite mixing or impurities in the core.
- **Core centering:** Hold the pencil up and inspect the cross-section when sharpened. A well-made pencil has its graphite core perfectly centered; off-center cores cause one side of the wood to shave away before the graphite is exposed.
- **Wood quality:** The casing should shave cleanly and evenly when sharpened. Cedar (the standard for quality pencils) sharpens without splintering or producing jagged edges.
- **Marking grade:** The grade marking (e.g., HB, 2B, 4H) should be clearly printed or embossed and match the pencil's actual performance.
- **Finish and lacquer:** Even, unpeeling paint with no bubbling or bare patches. Lacquer should be smooth, not sticky.
- **Eraser (if present):** Quality erasers are slightly tacky to the touch, erase cleanly without smearing, and do not leave heavy residue or tear paper.
- **Certifications:** Look for non-toxic certifications such as ACMI AP (Art & Creative Materials Institute Approved Product) seal on the barrel, which confirms the product has been toxicologically evaluated.

### Durability & Lifespan

- **Expected lifespan:** A standard HB pencil (~19 cm / 7.5 inches long) produces approximately 35–50 km (22–31 miles) of continuous line and can write roughly 45,000–50,000 words before being consumed entirely.
- **Under survival use** (intermittent writing, mapping, notes): a single pencil typically lasts 3–6 months of moderate daily use.
- **Factors that affect longevity:**
  - Over-sharpening (the most common cause of premature loss of usable length)
  - Pressure used while writing
  - Surface roughness (rough surfaces wear graphite faster)
  - Storage conditions (heat and dryness can cause the wood casing to crack)
- **Signs of degradation or failure:**
  - Core crumbling or cracking when sharpened (internal fractures from impact damage)
  - Wood casing splitting longitudinally
  - Eraser hardening, crumbling, or smearing instead of erasing cleanly
  - Lacquer peeling, which accelerates moisture absorption into the wood

## Maintenance & Repair

### Routine Maintenance

| Task | Frequency |
|------|-----------|
| Sharpen tip | As needed (typically every 1–3 pages of writing) |
| Clean eraser surface by rubbing on scrap paper | Weekly during regular use |
| Inspect core for cracks (visually at tip) | Every sharpening |
| Rotate pencil 90° in storage container to prevent flat-sided wear | Monthly |
| Check wooden casing for splits or swelling | Monthly |

### Common Repairs

| Issue | Solution | Difficulty | Required Tools |
|-------|----------|------------|----------------|
| Tip breakage (tip snaps off repeatedly) | Re-sharpen at a less acute angle; reduce writing pressure | Easy | Sharpener or knife |
| Core fracture inside barrel | Carefully sharpen incrementally — cracked sections will continue to break; use shorter strokes | Easy–Moderate | Sharpener or knife |
| Wood casing splitting lengthwise | Wrap split section tightly with electrical tape or thread; apply a drop of wood glue if available | Easy | Tape, wood glue (optional) |
| Eraser hardened and ineffective | Lightly sand eraser surface with fine sandpaper or rough stone to expose fresh material | Easy | Fine sandpaper or rough surface |
| Eraser leaving smear/residue | Replace eraser cap with a separate block eraser | Easy | Block eraser |
| Pencil too short to hold comfortably | Use a pencil extender/holder | Easy | Pencil extender (commercially available) |

### Required Consumables & Tools

- **Maintenance supplies:** Sharpener (manual pocket sharpener, manual crank sharpener, or field knife)
- **Repair tools:** Fine sandpaper (120–220 grit), electrical tape, wood glue
- **Replacement parts:** Eraser caps (slip-on replacement erasers), pencil extenders

## Storage Guidelines

### Optimal Storage Conditions

- **Temperature range:** 10–25°C (50–77°F). Avoid sustained temperatures above 40°C (104°F), which can dry out and crack wooden casings. Avoid freeze-thaw cycling if possible (though graphite itself tolerates freezing).
- **Humidity requirements:** 40–60% relative humidity. Excessive humidity (above 70%) causes the wood casing to swell, potentially cracking the graphite core; very low humidity (below 20%) causes the wood to dry, split, and warp.
- **Light exposure:** Pencils are not light-sensitive. Avoid prolonged UV exposure only to preserve label and paint finish.
- **Container or packaging recommendations:**
  - Store in a rigid case, tube, or sealed pouch to protect tips from breaking.
  - For long-term storage, a sealed plastic or metal tin is ideal.
  - Store horizontally rather than tip-down to prevent graphite core settling issues (relevant mainly for mechanical pencils and very soft grades).
  - Separate from items that produce moisture (e.g., don't store adjacent to water containers without sealed packaging).

### Degradation Prevention

1. **Protect tips during transport** using a hard case or individual tip caps to prevent core fractures that cause chronic breakage.
2. **Keep away from moisture** — wrap pencils in a sealed bag inside storage containers in humid environments or packs.
3. **Avoid storing near sustained heat sources** (campfires, vehicle dashboards in summer) which dry and split wood casings.
4. **Rubber bands around pencil bundles** can cause permanent indentations in soft wood casings — use elastic-free banding or a tube instead.
5. **Prevent graphite dust buildup** in storage containers by periodically wiping out containers, as accumulated dust can abrade pencil surfaces and contaminate other materials.

## Health & Safety Considerations

### Short-term Risks

- **Puncture injury:** A sharpened pencil tip can puncture skin, eyes, or soft tissue if mishandled. Graphite fragments embedded in skin ("pencil tattoo") can cause minor irritation and cosmetic discoloration but are generally not toxic.
- **Ingestion of small parts:** Eraser tips and broken pencil fragments pose a choking hazard for children under 3 years old.
- **Graphite dust inhalation:** Under normal use, exposure to graphite dust is negligible. Excessive generation of graphite dust (e.g., grinding pencils without ventilation) may cause mild respiratory irritation.
- **Chemical pigment exposure (colored pencils):** Some lower-quality colored pencils manufactured before modern safety standards may contain heavy metal-based pigments (cadmium, lead). Contact with skin is low-risk; ingestion is not.

### Long-term Health Impact

| Risk Factor | Potential Impact | Mitigation Strategy |
|-------------|------------------|---------------------|

## Annual Quantity Requirements

**For 1 person, 1 year:**

- **Consumable quantity:** 6–12 standard pencils per year (moderate daily use)
- **Survival/preparedness recommendation:** 24–36 pencils (2–3 dozen) per person per year, stored
- **Rationale:** Survival use includes navigation notation, map marking, journaling, signaling, resource lists, and communication. Pencils are consumed faster under field conditions (writing on rough surfaces, more sharpening, tip breakage). A buffer of 2–3× standard use accounts for loss, breakage, and sharing. Pencils are lightweight and inexpensive, making over-stocking low-cost. Supplementing with 1–2 mechanical pencils (with 10–20 spare lead refill packs) extends the supply effectively.

## Common Misconceptions

6. **Myth:** Pencils contain lead and are therefore toxic.
   **Reality:** Modern pencil cores are made of a mixture of graphite and clay — no lead whatsoever. The term "lead pencil" is a historical misnomer dating to 1565, when graphite deposits were first discovered and mistakenly identified as a form of lead (plumbago). Graphite is a non-toxic form of carbon.

7. **Myth:** Pencil writing is impermanent and will fade quickly, making it unreliable for long-term records.
   **Reality:** Graphite is chemically stable and does not fade the way ink does. Pencil writing on quality paper, stored away from heavy abrasion, can survive for centuries. Many historical documents, architectural drawings, and scientific records made in pencil remain legible after 100+ years.

8. **Myth:** A harder pencil (H grade) is always better for precision work.
   **Reality:** Harder grades (e.g., 4H, 6H) produce lighter, finer lines but can score or groove paper, making erasure incomplete. For survival writing and mapping, an HB or 2B is generally optimal — dark enough to be clearly legible, soft enough to not damage surfaces.

9. **Myth:** Mechanical pencils are superior to wooden pencils in all survival scenarios.
   **Reality:** Mechanical pencils require lead refills and functioning advance mechanisms. If the mechanism breaks or refills run out, the pencil is useless. Wooden pencils can be sharpened with any blade and have no mechanical parts to fail, making them more robust in field conditions.

10. **Myth:** Pencils cannot write on wet surfaces.
   **Reality:** Graphite adheres to surfaces through mechanical bonding, not chemical reaction like ink. Pencils can write on damp paper, stone, wood, and other surfaces where a ballpoint pen would skip. Dedicated "all-weather" pencils (with harder casings and higher graphite-to-clay ratios) perform even better in wet conditions.

11. **Myth:** A broken pencil is useless without a sharpener.
   **Reality:** Any sharp blade — a knife, a piece of flint, a rough rock — can be used to whittle and expose the graphite core for writing. This is a basic and important fieldcraft skill. The graphite core of a broken pencil can also be used to write directly, held in the fingers, even without a wooden casing.

## DIY / Manufacturing Guide

### Feasibility

**Can be made at home:** Partially — functional field pencils (exposed graphite sticks or graphite wrapped in paper) can be made at home. A fully finished wooden-cased pencil matching commercial quality requires industrial tooling and is not practically achievable in home or survival settings.

### Instructions (if feasible)

**Option A: Paper-wrapped graphite pencil (field-expedient)**

12. Obtain a graphite stick or break apart a commercial pencil to extract the graphite core.
13. Cut a strip of paper approximately 15 cm × 4 cm (6 in × 1.5 in).
14. Place the graphite core at one edge of the paper strip.
15. Roll the paper tightly and evenly around the graphite, ensuring the core is centered.
16. Secure the roll with tape, thread, or glue.
17. Leave approximately 3–5 mm of graphite exposed at one end.
18. The resulting pencil can be used immediately. Re-expose graphite by peeling back paper as the tip wears.

**Option B: Wooden-cased pencil (partial, simplified)**

19. Split a straight-grained soft wood stick (cedar is ideal) into two equal halves, approximately 19 cm long.
20. Carve a shallow channel the length of one half, wide enough to seat the graphite core (1.8–3 mm).
21. Place the graphite rod (obtained from a commercial mechanical pencil lead pack or broken pencil) in the channel.
22. Apply wood glue along the channel edge.
23. Press the second half firmly over the first, aligning the groove to encase the graphite.
24. Clamp or bind tightly with cord and allow to cure for 24 hours.
25. Once dry, whittle and sand the exterior to a comfortable round or hexagonal cross-section.
26. Sharpen with a knife to expose the graphite tip.

**Required Materials:**
- Graphite rod or commercial pencil lead (1.8–3 mm diameter)
- Straight-grained soft wood (cedar, pine, or basswood preferred)
- Wood glue or hide glue
- Paper (for Option A)
- Tape or binding cord

**Required Tools:**
- Sharp knife or chisel
- Fine sandpaper (120–220 grit)
- Clamps or strong binding cord
- Ruler

**Estimated Time:** 1–2 hours (excluding 24-hour curing time for wood glue)

**Safety Notes:** Use extreme care when whittling wood and carving channels — cuts should always be directed away from the body. Graphite rods are brittle; handle carefully to avoid breakage during assembly.

### Why Not Full DIY

Manufacturing the graphite-clay core to commercial specifications requires precision mixing of graphite powder and clay at controlled ratios, extrusion through industrial dies to exact diameters, kiln-firing at temperatures of approximately 900–1200°C (1650–2200°F), and precision wood-cutting and grooving machinery for the casing. These processes require industrial equipment not available in home or survival settings. The simplified field methods described above produce functional but lower-quality writing instruments adequate for emergency use only.
