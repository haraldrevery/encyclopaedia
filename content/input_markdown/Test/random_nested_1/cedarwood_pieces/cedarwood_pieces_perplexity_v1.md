# Cedarwood Pieces

## Overview

Cedarwood pieces are small blocks, chips, cubes, rings, or granules made from aromatic cedar, most often eastern red cedar, which naturally emits insect‑repellent and deodorizing oils. In survival and emergency preparedness, they are used to protect stored clothing and gear from moths and other insects, reduce musty odors and moisture in containers, and provide dry, resinous kindling for fire starting when other tinder is scarce. Their long‑lasting, low‑maintenance nature makes them a compact, multi‑use addition to long‑term storage bins and bug‑out kits.[^1][^2][^3][^4][^5]

***

## Usage \& Safety Protocols

### How to Use

1. **As an insect‑repellent in clothing and gear storage**

2. Place several cedarwood pieces evenly throughout enclosed spaces such as clothing bins, rucksacks, lockers, or footlockers where textiles or gear will be stored for weeks to months.[^5][^1]
3. Ensure the space is as sealed as practical (tight‑fitting lid, zipper, or drawstring) so volatile oils accumulate enough to repel fabric‑destroying moths and similar pests.[^6][^7][^1]
4. Keep pieces off delicate fabrics if they feel resinous; place them in mesh bags or cotton sachets to avoid direct contact with light‑colored or oil‑sensitive materials.[^8][^5]
5. Every so often, open the container briefly for inspection and to check scent strength; if aroma has faded, lightly sand the surface of each block and return to storage.[^7][^9][^10]
1. **As a deodorizer and moisture buffer in small enclosures**

2. Place cedarwood pieces in boots, small gear pouches, footlockers, and other confined spaces to reduce musty odors and help moderate moisture around organic materials.[^11][^1]
3. Combine with basic moisture control (desiccant packs, ventilation as needed) rather than relying on cedar alone to prevent mold.[^12][^13]
4. Rotate pieces between items (e.g., from boots to packs) to spread their effect across your kit.[^5]
1. **As kindling / tinder in fire starting**

2. Shave thin curls or splinters from the edge of a dry cedar block with a knife to create fine tinder; gather a small handful.[^3]
3. Build a small teepee or log‑cabin of dry twigs and branches, placing the cedar shavings in the center as your primary ignition material.[^14][^3]
4. Ignite the shavings with a match, lighter, or spark (ferro rod), then gradually feed larger kindling and fuelwood; cedar’s resinous softwood fibers light more easily than many hardwoods when dry.[^14][^3]
5. Reserve some cedar pieces exclusively as emergency tinder and protect them from moisture in a sealed bag or tin inside your kit.[^12]
1. **Optional: Enhancing repellent effect with cedar oil (fixed location use)**

2. In a fixed shelter or homestead (not in a confined bug‑out bag), you may apply a small amount of commercial cedarwood oil to blocks to intensify aroma, keeping in mind that high, chronic exposures have shown toxicity in animal and occupational studies.[^15][^16][^17]
3. Allow treated blocks to dry in a ventilated area before placing them in enclosed storage to avoid excessive vapor buildup.[^16][^18]

### Safety Guidelines

- **Critical safety warnings**
    - Cedar dust from cutting or sanding can irritate eyes and airways and is a well‑recognized cause of occupational asthma in workers exposed to western red cedar dust due to plicatic acid.[^19][^20][^21][^22]
    - High‑dose, long‑term exposure to concentrated cedarwood oil has been associated with liver and lung toxicity in animal and worker studies, though normal consumer use at low levels is considered unlikely to pose “unreasonable adverse effects” by regulatory reviews.[^23][^17][^15][^16]
    - Cedar shavings and bedding have been linked to skin and respiratory irritation in small animals and may exacerbate respiratory disease in sensitive pets and laboratory species.[^24][^25]
- **Proper handling procedures**
    - When sanding or cutting cedarwood pieces indoors, wear eye protection and at least a dust mask or respirator, and work in a well‑ventilated area to reduce inhalation of dust and volatile compounds.[^21][^22][^19]
    - Avoid prolonged close‑range inhalation of concentrated cedar aromas in very small, poorly ventilated spaces, especially for individuals with asthma, chronic lung disease, or strong fragrance sensitivities.[^26][^20][^19]
    - Do not use cedarwood pieces as bedding for small animals or line cages for rodents and similar pets, as phenols and acids in cedar can irritate airways and skin over time.[^25][^24]
    - Keep small blocks and cubes away from infants and toddlers who may mouth or chew them.
- **Emergency protocols**
    - If someone develops wheezing, chest tightness, or severe coughing after exposure to cedar dust or strong vapors, move them to fresh air and seek medical attention; plicatic‑acid–induced asthma can persist even after exposure stops in susceptible individuals.[^20][^22][^19]
    - In case of eye contact with dust or liquid cedar oil, rinse thoroughly with clean water; persistent irritation or vision changes require medical evaluation.[^18]
    - If a pet develops respiratory distress, lethargy, or skin irritation after exposure to cedar bedding or strong cedar vapors, remove the source and consult a veterinarian.[^24][^25]
- **Common beginner mistakes to avoid**
    - Assuming cedar will **kill** existing insect infestations; in reality it primarily repels and does not reliably kill eggs or larvae already present in fabrics or containers.[^27][^28][^7]
    - Relying on cedar alone without cleaning, sealing containers, or controlling humidity, which can still allow moths, beetles, or mold to damage items.[^13][^7][^12]
    - Sanding cedar blocks in a closet or small room without dust control or respiratory protection.[^9][^19][^21]
    - Using **any** cedar species interchangeably; aromatic eastern red cedar has a strong scent suitable for closets, whereas western red cedar has little aroma but similar dust‑related asthma risks.[^10][^4]


### Practice \& Skill Building

To build skill, practice deploying cedarwood pieces systematically in a non‑critical setting: outfit a seasonal clothing bin or gear trunk, track pest damage and odor over several months, and note how many pieces are needed to maintain a noticeable scent in that volume. Periodically sand a subset of blocks and compare repellency and aroma to unsanded ones so you learn how much resurfacing is needed for your specific storage conditions.[^1][^7][^9][^10][^5]

When practicing fire‑starting, reserve a small number of cedar pieces solely as tinder and experiment in safe, controlled conditions with producing shavings and lighting them using different ignition sources (matches, lighters, ferro rods) so you understand how fine the shavings must be and how dry they need to stay. Track any respiratory or skin irritation during sanding or heavy use; if symptoms appear, practice alternative storage or fire‑starting methods and treat cedarwood pieces as a limited‑use item rather than a primary tool.[^22][^19][^20][^3][^14]

## Quality Assessment

### Quality Indicators

- **Wood species and aroma**
    - High‑quality pieces are typically made from **aromatic eastern red cedar** (*Juniperus virginiana*), known for a strong, pleasant scent and a higher density than western red cedar; it is widely used for closets, chests, and wardrobes.[^4][^10]
    - A noticeable, woodsy aroma indicates active essential oils, including compounds such as thujone, cedrol, and related sesquiterpenes that are associated with insect‑repellent activity.[^29][^6][^27]
- **Appearance and machining**
    - Fresh pieces usually show red to purplish heartwood with contrasting pale sapwood, a typical visual hallmark of aromatic cedar.[^10][^4]
    - Smoothly cut edges and faces without deep splinters, resin globs, or large cracks indicate better machining and handling; many commercial closet blocks are finished to a uniform thickness around 0.5–0.75 inches for smooth handling.[^30][^5]
- **Labeling and intended use**
    - Retail products sold for closets and garment storage are often labeled as 100% natural aromatic cedar or eastern red cedar and marketed as safe around families and pets when used as directed.[^2][^11][^5]
    - Some sets include small sachets or sandpaper specifically for refreshing the scent, indicating the manufacturer expects long‑term use and periodic maintenance rather than disposability.[^9][^30][^8]


### Durability \& Lifespan

- **Expected lifespan under normal use**
    - The wood itself is naturally rot‑ and decay‑resistant due to compounds such as plicatic acid and related phenolics, and cedar lumber is used for exterior siding, poles, and decking, demonstrating long service life when kept reasonably dry.[^31][^25]
    - In indoor storage and closet applications, physical cedar blocks can remain structurally sound for many years; their functional lifespan is limited more by aroma loss than by mechanical breakdown.[^7][^9][^10]
- **Factors that affect longevity**
    - High temperature and strong air circulation accelerate evaporation of volatile oils, reducing scent and repellent effect faster.[^26][^12]
    - Elevated relative humidity encourages mold growth on any organic surface, including cedar, once moisture levels exceed the thresholds (roughly above 70–80% RH for extended periods) favorable to fungi.[^32][^33][^13]
    - Frequent sanding refreshes aroma but gradually removes material; light, infrequent resurfacing typically balances scent renewal with long‑term durability.[^34][^9][^10]
- **Signs of degradation or failure to watch for**
    - Very faint or absent aroma even after light sanding suggests most volatile oils near the surface have been exhausted; at that point blocks still function as general wood but offer little repellent effect.[^7][^9]
    - Soft, spongy areas, visible mold, or deep discoloration indicate moisture damage; such pieces are poor candidates for enclosed storage and may need to be discarded.[^33][^32]
    - Severe cracking and splintering make pieces harder to handle safely but generally do not affect their performance as tinder.

## Maintenance \& Repair

### Routine Maintenance

- Lightly dust or wipe cedarwood pieces with a dry or slightly damp cloth a few times per year, especially if they are exposed on shelves or in open closets.[^9][^10]
- When the scent has noticeably faded, lightly sand each face with fine or medium‑grit sandpaper until fresh reddish wood is exposed, then wipe off dust and allow the block to dry before returning it to service.[^34][^10][^9]
- In very dry climates, an optional, thin application of cedarwood oil can be buffed into the surface of closet linings or blocks in fixed installations, but this should be used sparingly and with ventilation to avoid excessive vapor exposure.[^16][^10]


### Common Repairs

| Issue | Solution | Difficulty | Required Tools |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Aroma has faded | Lightly sand all faces to expose fresh wood, wipe off dust, and let dry before reuse.[^9][^10] | Easy | Fine/medium sandpaper, cloth |
| Surface is rough or splintery | Sand edges and faces until smooth; remove loose splinters and rounded corners.[^9][^30] | Easy | Sandpaper, dust mask |
| Light mold or mildew spots on surface | Move blocks to a dry, ventilated area; allow to dry thoroughly, then sand away surface growth and discard any pieces with deep penetration.[^32][^33] | Moderate | Sandpaper, brush, PPE |
| Block cracked but still solid | Continue use for enclosed storage if safe to handle, or relegate to fire‑starting tinder; optionally trim into smaller pieces.[^3] | Easy | Saw or knife (optional) |
| Excessive cedar dust accumulation | Vacuum or wipe storage area; handle future sanding outdoors or with dust collection to reduce buildup.[^19][^21][^9] | Easy | Vacuum, mask, cloth |

### Required Consumables \& Tools

- **Maintenance supplies**
    - Fine to medium‑grit sandpaper for resurfacing and scent renewal.[^10][^9]
    - Clean dry or slightly damp cloths for wiping dust.
    - Optional, small quantity of cedarwood oil for refreshing fixed closets or blocks in well‑ventilated, non‑emergency locations.[^16][^10]
- **Repair tools**
    - Hand saw or small saw for trimming or shaping pieces if desired.
    - Dust mask or respirator and eye protection when sanding or cutting, particularly for individuals with respiratory sensitivity.[^19][^21][^22]
- **Replacement parts**
    - Additional cedarwood pieces or boards (aromatic eastern red cedar) to cut new blocks when older ones are too small, heavily cracked, or depleted of useful aroma.[^4][^10]

## Storage Guidelines

### Optimal Storage Conditions

- **Temperature range**
    - Store cedarwood pieces at normal indoor or temperate conditions; typical storage temperatures for belongings (roughly 5–30 °C) are compatible with preserving wood and volatile oils.[^13][^12]
- **Humidity requirements**
    - Aim to keep storage relative humidity below about 60%, ideally in the 30–50% range recommended for general indoor environments to limit mold growth and moisture damage to stored goods.[^35][^12][^13]
    - Avoid long‑term storage in spaces that regularly exceed 70–80% relative humidity, as this range supports mold development on wood and other organics.[^32][^33]
- **Light exposure**
    - Store away from direct, strong sunlight to minimize surface drying and cracking; indirect light or darkness inside bins, packs, and closets is preferred.
    - UV exposure mainly affects surface color rather than function, but limiting it helps keep blocks in better overall condition.
- **Container or packaging recommendations**
    - For long‑term emergency storage, keep spare cedarwood pieces in breathable cotton bags or mesh pouches inside sealed plastic bins so aroma is preserved while dust is contained.[^8][^5]
    - When used actively in closets or chests, place pieces where air can circulate around them (corners, shelves, shoe interiors) rather than sealing them inside plastic, which traps oils but limits contact with the surrounding air.[^36][^1]


### Degradation Prevention

- Keep overall storage humidity within recommended ranges by using desiccant packs or dehumidifiers in damp environments, reducing the risk of mold on both cedar and surrounding gear.[^33][^12][^13]
- Periodically inspect storage containers and closets for condensation, visible mold, or musty odors, and dry the space promptly if problems appear.[^35][^13]
- Avoid coating cedarwood pieces with varnish, polyurethane, or other sealants, as these block the release of essential oils and defeat their repellent and deodorizing purpose.[^9][^10]
- Store spare blocks in a dry, ventilated area separate from chemicals or strong odors that might be absorbed into the wood.

## Health \& Safety Considerations

### Short-term Risks

Short‑term exposure to cedar dust during sanding or cutting can cause irritation of the eyes, skin, and respiratory tract, especially in people with pre‑existing asthma or allergies. In some individuals, even brief high‑level exposure may trigger wheezing and bronchospasm consistent with occupational asthma related to plicatic acid in western red cedar.[^37][^20][^21][^22][^19]

Topical or inhalation exposure to high concentrations of cedarwood oil has shown limited but notable toxicity in animal studies, including respiratory irritation and potential pulmonary damage at high doses, although human risk under normal low‑level consumer use is considered low by regulatory reviews. Pets and small animals may experience acute skin and respiratory irritation when exposed to cedar shavings or strong vapors, and such bedding has been discouraged in veterinary and small‑animal care resources.[^17][^25][^15][^23][^24][^16]

### Long-term Health Impact

| Risk Factor | Potential Impact | Mitigation Strategy |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Chronic inhalation of cedar dust (occupational‑like) | Development of western red cedar asthma, chronic cough, wheeze, and persistent airway hyper‑responsiveness in a subset of workers (about 4–13.5% of those exposed in studies).[^19][^20][^22] | Avoid routine power‑sanding indoors; use dust extraction, respirators, and limit sanding sessions.[^19][^21][^37] |
| Repeated high‑level exposure to cedarwood oil | Liver and pulmonary toxicity observed in rodent and worker studies at high, long‑term doses; current registered uses judged low‑risk at normal consumer exposure levels.[^15][^23][^16][^17] | Use oils sparingly, avoid saturating small unventilated spaces, and follow product labeling; avoid use in pregnancy if concerned. |
| Use of cedar bedding for small animals | Chronic respiratory disease, skin irritation, and increased risk of infection reported in small animals exposed to cedar shavings.[^24][^25] | Avoid cedar bedding for rodents and small pets; use inert substrates; keep cedar blocks out of animal enclosures. |
| Hormonal / fertility effects in humans (theoretical) | No clear human data linking typical cedarwood exposure to endocrine disruption or fertility issues; toxicology work has focused on irritation, organ toxicity, and asthma.[^15][^23][^38] | Treat hormonal and fertility concerns as *theoretical*; minimize unnecessary high‑dose oil use, especially for pregnant people and those with chronic illness. |
| Material toxicity to children and elderly | At normal household and storage uses, cedarwood blocks are generally described as safe around families and pets when used as directed, though sensitive individuals may react to dust or strong fragrances.[^2][^5][^16] | Keep blocks out of reach of mouthing infants, avoid sanding near vulnerable individuals, and monitor for respiratory or skin reactions. |

## Common Misconceptions

1. **Myth:** “Cedarwood pieces kill moths, larvae, and all insects in storage.”
**Reality:** Cedar primarily acts as a **repellent**; it discourages insects and interferes with their ability to locate fabrics, but it does not reliably kill existing eggs or larvae, so heavily infested gear still requires cleaning or other control methods.[^28][^27][^7]
2. **Myth:** “If I can’t smell the cedar, it isn’t working at all.”
**Reality:** Repellent compounds can persist at levels too low for humans to smell, but as aroma fades, efficacy drops; sanding or refreshing the surface is needed for consistent protection, especially in larger or leakier spaces.[^7][^10][^9]
3. **Myth:** “Any type of cedar board works just like aromatic cedar closet blocks.”
**Reality:** Aromatic eastern red cedar has a strong scent and is specifically used for closets, while western red cedar has much less aroma yet still carries similar respiratory‑sensitizing dust; choosing the correct aromatic species matters for repellency.[^4][^10]
4. **Myth:** “Cedar is completely harmless at any level of exposure because it’s natural.”
**Reality:** Occupational studies document asthma from cedar dust, and high‑dose cedarwood oil exposure has caused organ toxicity in animals; regulatory agencies consider normal consumer uses low‑risk but not completely risk‑free.[^15][^22][^19][^16]
5. **Myth:** “Cedar blocks alone will keep stored gear mold‑free.”
**Reality:** While cedar may help reduce odors and some microbial growth, mold prevention is driven mainly by controlling humidity and moisture; high relative humidity can still support mold on wood and fabrics regardless of cedar’s presence.[^32][^12][^13][^33]
6. **Myth:** “Once installed, cedar closets and blocks never need maintenance.”
**Reality:** The volatile oils that provide scent and repellency slowly evaporate; light sanding and occasional refreshing with cedar oil (in ventilated conditions) are standard practices to maintain performance over the years.[^42][^34][^10][^9]

## DIY / Manufacturing Guide

### Feasibility

**Can be made at home:** Yes

Aromatic cedar boards (especially eastern red cedar) are commercially available and are routinely used to line closets and build chests, so cutting them down into small blocks or rings is technically straightforward for anyone with basic woodworking tools.[^31][^10][^4]

### Instructions (if feasible)

1. **Select material**

2. Obtain an aromatic eastern red cedar board or off‑cuts suitable for indoor use; verify by scent and appearance (reddish heartwood, strong aroma).[^10][^4]
1. **Plan dimensions**

2. Mark out small rectangles roughly 7 × 5 × 1–1.5 cm (or to match common commercial block sizes) on the board, adjusting to your scrap sizes.[^30][^5]
1. **Cut pieces**

2. Using a hand saw or power saw, cut along the marked lines to create individual blocks; wear eye protection and a dust mask to reduce inhalation of cedar dust.[^21][^22][^19]
1. **Shape and smooth**

2. Lightly round sharp corners and edges with sandpaper to prevent splinters and snagging on fabric.[^30][^9]
3. Sand faces until smooth; stop once fresh reddish wood is exposed and aroma is strong.
1. **Optional: Add features**

2. Drill small holes near an edge if you want to thread cord or hang blocks on hooks in closets or gear lockers.
3. Optionally stamp or mark blocks to track batches or intended uses (e.g., “tinder only”).
1. **Clean and deploy**

2. Wipe each block with a dry or slightly damp cloth to remove dust, then allow to fully dry.
3. Place blocks into storage containers, closets, or gear as described in the usage section.

**Required Materials:**

- Aromatic eastern red cedar board or off‑cuts.[^4][^10]
- Optional cotton bags or mesh pouches if you prefer to avoid direct contact with clothing.[^8][^5]

**Required Tools:**

- Measuring tape or ruler and pencil.
- Hand saw or power saw suitable for small cuts.
- Sandpaper (medium and fine grit).
- Eye protection and dust mask or respirator for cutting and sanding.[^22][^19][^21]
- Optional drill and small bits (for hanging holes).

**Estimated Time:** N/A

**Safety Notes:**
Cut and sand only in well‑ventilated areas or outdoors, using appropriate respiratory and eye protection to reduce the risk of cedar‑dust‑related irritation or asthma, especially if you have any history of respiratory disease. Keep power tools away from children, secure workpieces to avoid slips, and collect or vacuum cedar dust to prevent accumulation in living spaces, which can increase inhalation exposure.[^20][^19][^21][^22][^9]

### Why Not DIY (if not feasible)

N/A (DIY production is generally feasible with basic woodworking skills and safety precautions; the main limitations are access to aromatic cedar lumber and proper dust control.)
