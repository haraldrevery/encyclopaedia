# T-Shirt

## Overview

A T-shirt is a short-sleeved, collarless garment typically made from knit fabric (most commonly cotton or a cotton blend), named for its T-shaped silhouette when laid flat. In survival and emergency preparedness contexts, T-shirts serve as a foundational base layer providing thermal regulation, moisture management, and skin protection from environmental hazards such as sun exposure, abrasion, and insects. Their lightweight and compact nature makes them a high-priority item in bug-out bags and long-term clothing stockpiles.

---

## Usage & Safety Protocols

### How to Use

1. **Select the appropriate size.** Lay the shirt flat and measure chest width across the armpits; double this value and compare to your chest circumference. A proper fit should allow one to two fingers of slack at the side seam.
2. **Orient the shirt correctly.** Identify the front panel (typically features a seam or tag at the back collar) and the back panel.
3. **Put on the shirt.** Grip the bottom hem with both hands, lift over the head, and feed your arms through the sleeves simultaneously. Pull the hem down to your waistline.
4. **Tuck or leave untucked** as appropriate to the situation. In tick- or insect-heavy environments, tuck the shirt into pants and cinch the waistband.
5. **Layer if needed.** In cold conditions, wear the T-shirt as a base layer directly against skin beneath a mid-layer fleece or long-sleeve shirt.
6. **For improvised use** (e.g., bandage, water pre-filter, signal flag), remove the shirt and repurpose as needed; prioritise skin protection when deciding to sacrifice the garment.
7. **Rotate garments.** In multi-day scenarios, air out worn shirts and rotate to a dry replacement to prevent skin breakdown.

### Safety Guidelines

- **Sun protection:** Standard cotton T-shirts offer approximately UPF 5–10 when dry and may drop to near zero when wet. In high UV environments, use a long-sleeved shirt or apply sunscreen to exposed skin beneath the shirt fabric.
- **Do not wear wet T-shirts in cold conditions** without an insulating outer layer; evaporative cooling accelerates hypothermia.
- **Avoid synthetic shirts near open flame** (e.g., campfires, emergency heating). Polyester and nylon melt and bond to skin, causing severe burns. Wool or cotton is preferred in fire-adjacent situations.
- **Loose weave shirts** may not provide adequate protection against mosquito bites; treat with permethrin in insect-heavy areas.
- **Do not use T-shirts as tourniquets without modification.** Standard knit fabric stretches and cannot maintain adequate arterial pressure; use a rigid stick or rod as a windlass with fabric strips cut from the shirt.
- **Beginner mistake:** Wearing a single cotton T-shirt as the only layer in cold/wet conditions. Cotton loses nearly all insulating value when wet; always carry a synthetic or wool alternative.
- **Emergency protocol:** If a T-shirt is used as a wound dressing, apply direct pressure only. Do not remove a blood-soaked shirt from a wound — layer additional material on top and maintain pressure until professional care is available.

### Practice & Skill Building

- **Improvised bandage practice:** Cut old shirts into strips and practice making pressure bandages and slings on a mannequin or training partner.
- **Fire-risk awareness:** Hold a small swatch of polyester fabric near (not in) a flame to observe melt behaviour vs. cotton char behaviour.
- **Improvised water pre-filter:** Practice pouring water through multiple folded layers of a T-shirt into a container to remove visible particulates before chemical or boiling treatment.
- **Common beginner mistakes:**
  - Selecting cotton-only shirts for all-weather preparedness without understanding its wet-insulation failure.
  - Overpacking identical shirts without diversity of fabric weight or sleeve length.
  - Failing to account for garment drying time in kit rotation planning.

## Quality Assessment

### Quality Indicators

- **Fabric weight:** Premium T-shirts typically use 180–220 g/m² (gsm) fabric. Below 140 gsm indicates lightweight/disposable quality; above 240 gsm indicates heavyweight durability.
- **Stitch type:** Overlock (serger) stitching on seams indicates better durability than straight-stitch seams. Run a finger along inner seams — double-needle hems are flat and symmetrical.
- **Yarn quality:** Ring-spun cotton is smoother, stronger, and more durable than open-end (OE) spun cotton. The shirt will feel noticeably softer and have less surface pilling.
- **Combed cotton:** Combed cotton removes short fibres before spinning, resulting in a denser, more uniform yarn.
- **Colorfastness:** Wet a small, inconspicuous area with water and press against a white cloth. Significant colour transfer indicates poor dye quality.
- **Shrinkage pre-treatment:** Tags reading "pre-shrunk" or "preshrunk" indicate the fabric has been treated to reduce post-wash shrinkage; verify with brand specifications.
- **Label content:** Labels should list fabric composition clearly (e.g., "100% cotton" or "60% cotton / 40% polyester"). Avoid garments without composition labels.
- **Seam reinforcement:** Reinforced shoulder and side seams (visible as a double-stitched line) indicate higher-tier construction.

### Durability & Lifespan

- **Expected lifespan under normal use:** 50–200 wash cycles, or approximately 2–5 years of regular wear, depending on fabric weight, wash temperature, and drying method.
- **Factors affecting longevity:**
  - High-heat machine drying accelerates fibre degradation and shrinkage.
  - Chlorine bleach weakens cotton fibres significantly.
  - Abrasive washing surfaces and high spin speeds cause pilling and seam stress.
  - UV exposure from outdoor storage or line drying in direct sun causes colour fading and fibre weakening over time.
- **Signs of degradation:**
  - Fabric transparency (thinning); hold the shirt up to light — visible through worn areas.
  - Pilling (surface fibre balls), particularly on friction areas (underarms, sides).
  - Seam unravelling or holes at collar and hem stress points.
  - Persistent odour that does not wash out (biofilm in fibres).
  - Significant dimensional change (more than ~5% shrinkage from original).

## Maintenance & Repair

### Routine Maintenance

- **After each wear:** Air out the shirt on a hanger or dry line. Do not store damp or compressed.
- **Washing frequency:** Wash after 1–3 wears under normal conditions; immediately after heavy sweating, contact with contaminants, or visible soiling.
- **Washing method:** Machine wash cold or warm (≤40°C / 104°F) on a gentle cycle. Cold water preserves colour and reduces shrinkage. Turn inside out to protect printed graphics.
- **Drying method:** Line dry in shade or tumble dry on low heat. Avoid high heat which accelerates fibre fatigue.
- **Folding/storage:** Fold flat or roll for compact storage. Do not hang stored shirts for extended periods; this stretches the collar and shoulder area.
- **Annual review:** Inspect all garments for seam integrity, fabric thinning, and elasticity loss at collar and cuffs.

### Common Repairs

| Issue | Solution | Difficulty | Required Tools |
|-------|----------|------------|----------------|
| Small hole in fabric body | Iron-on fusible patch (inside) or hand-stitch with backstitch | Easy | Needle, thread or fusible patch, iron |

### Required Consumables & Tools

- **Maintenance supplies:** Mild detergent (fragrance-free preferred for skin sensitivity), colour-safe fabric softener (optional), stain pre-treatment spray
- **Repair tools:** Hand-sewing needles (sizes 7–9 sharps), thread in matching colours (cotton or cotton/poly blend), sewing machine (optional), seam ripper, iron, pressing cloth
- **Replacement parts:** Iron-on patches (woven fabric type for durability), fusible webbing tape, elastic collar band tape

## Storage Guidelines

### Optimal Storage Conditions

- **Temperature range:** 10–25°C (50–77°F). Avoid prolonged storage above 35°C, which degrades elastic fibres in blends and accelerates yellowing of white cotton.
- **Humidity requirements:** 40–60% relative humidity. High humidity (>70%) promotes mildew growth in folded cotton. Low humidity (<30%) can cause static buildup in synthetic blends.
- **Light exposure:** Avoid direct sunlight and UV light. Store in opaque containers or a dark closet. Prolonged UV exposure causes colour fading and fibre weakening, particularly in dyed and printed shirts.
- **Container or packaging recommendations:** Fold and store in breathable cotton bags, cardboard boxes, or sealed plastic bins with a silica gel desiccant packet. Vacuum-sealed bags are acceptable for long-term storage but avoid for daily-use rotation, as repeated compression degrades fibre resilience. Do not store in airtight plastic without desiccant; trapped moisture causes mildew.

### Degradation Prevention

- **Wash and dry thoroughly** before storage; body oils and residual moisture accelerate fabric yellowing and mildew.
- **Add desiccant:** Place one or two silica gel packets per storage container to absorb ambient moisture.
- **Use cedar blocks or lavender sachets** as a natural insect deterrent; avoid mothballs (naphthalene) which leave lasting odour and may off-gas into fabric.
- **Rotate stock:** Use a first-in, first-out rotation so that older garments are used before newer ones, preventing any single shirt from sitting undisturbed for years.
- **Avoid direct ground contact** in storage areas prone to flooding or moisture wicking.
- **For white shirts:** Store away from coloured garments to prevent dye transfer, particularly in humid conditions.

## Health & Safety Considerations

### Short-term Risks

- **Contact dermatitis:** Dye residue in new garments, particularly dark colours, can cause skin irritation in sensitive individuals. Wash new T-shirts before first wear.
- **Formaldehyde in wrinkle-resistant finishes:** Some "easy care" or "wrinkle-free" cotton shirts are treated with formaldehyde-based resins. These can cause skin irritation and respiratory symptoms, particularly in people with chemical sensitivities. Wash thoroughly before first use.
- **Overheating:** Wearing heavyweight cotton in high-heat environments may trap heat and contribute to heat exhaustion if worn without adequate hydration and rest.
- **Hypothermia risk:** Wearing wet cotton in cold environments is a well-documented hypothermia risk factor; see Safety Guidelines.
- **Allergic reactions:** Latex-containing elastic trims or nickel-containing snaps/labels can cause localised allergic reactions. Examine labels for nickel-free or latex-free claims if sensitivity is known.

### Long-term Health Impact

| Risk Factor | Potential Impact | Mitigation Strategy |
|-------------|------------------|---------------------|
| Formaldehyde resin (wrinkle-free treatments) — *documented* | Skin irritation, contact dermatitis; classified as a known human carcinogen by IARC at high inhalation doses; textile exposure at low level is a *theoretical* systemic concern | Wash garments before first wear; choose untreated or OEKO-TEX certified fabrics; avoid daily wear of "wrinkle-resistant" shirts without washing |
| Synthetic dyes (azo dyes) — *documented at high exposure* | Certain azo dyes can release carcinogenic aromatic amines on decomposition; documented risk in occupational textile workers; consumer skin-contact risk is *theoretical* at normal wear levels | Choose OEKO-TEX Standard 100 or GOTS-certified garments; wash before first wear |
| Microplastic release from synthetic blends (polyester, nylon) — *documented* | Microplastics shed during washing have been documented in waterways; ingestion/inhalation effects are under active research and considered *theoretical* for wearers at this time | Use a microplastic-catching laundry bag (e.g., Guppyfriend); wash at lower temperatures; prefer natural fibre shirts |
| Pesticide residues in non-organic cotton — *theoretical at consumer level* | Conventional cotton is a heavily pesticide-treated crop; residue in finished fabric is typically low but not zero; long-term dermal absorption is *theoretical* | Choose GOTS-certified organic cotton for individuals with chemical sensitivities or for children's garments |
| UV radiation inadequacy — *documented* | Relying on a standard T-shirt as sun protection without supplementary measures can lead to chronic UV exposure; associated with long-term skin cancer risk | Use UPF-rated garments or apply broad-spectrum sunscreen; prefer tightly woven, darker-coloured fabrics for outdoor use |
| Skin microbiome disruption from antibacterial treatments — *theoretical* | Antimicrobial silver-ion or triclosan-treated fabrics may affect skin flora with repeated contact; systemic significance is not yet established | Avoid antimicrobial-treated garments for everyday baseline use unless specifically needed |

**Population notes:**
- **Children:** More susceptible to dye and chemical residue exposure due to higher skin surface-area-to-body-weight ratio and hand-to-mouth behaviour. Prioritise organic, certified fabrics.
- **Pregnant individuals:** As a precautionary measure, avoid formaldehyde-treated and heavily dyed garments, particularly in the first trimester.
- **Elderly:** Reduced skin barrier function increases sensitivity to dye and chemical irritants; choose plain, pre-washed, certified natural fibre garments.

## Annual Quantity Requirements

**For 1 person, 1 year:**

- **If durable:** 5–10 T-shirts recommended (with rationale below)
- **Rationale:** A standard rotation for one person engaged in moderate physical activity requires approximately 5–7 T-shirts to allow a daily change, washing and drying time (1–2 days per garment per cycle), and 1–2 spares for damage or improvised use. In an extended emergency scenario without laundry access, a minimum of 7 shirts allows a 7-day rotation before re-wearing. Add 2–3 additional shirts as reserves for fabric repurposing (bandaging, pre-filtering, signalling). 10 shirts is the recommended upper bound for a well-rounded 1-year preparedness stock per person.

## Common Misconceptions

8. **Myth:** Cotton is always the best fabric for a T-shirt in all survival situations.
   **Reality:** Cotton is highly comfortable and breathable in dry, warm conditions but is dangerous in cold or wet environments because it absorbs water and loses virtually all insulating value when wet. Wool and synthetic blends retain far more warmth when damp. A preparedness kit should include garments in multiple fabric types suited to different environmental scenarios.

9. **Myth:** A T-shirt provides meaningful sun protection on its own.
   **Reality:** A standard white cotton T-shirt has a UPF rating of approximately 5–8 when dry — far below the UPF 30+ recommended for adequate sun protection. Wet fabric drops even further. High-UV environments require UPF-rated shirts, shade, or supplementary sunscreen.

10. **Myth:** T-shirts can be used as effective tourniquets in their existing form.
   **Reality:** Standard knit T-shirt fabric stretches significantly under tension, making it unsuitable as a direct tourniquet without modification. A T-shirt can be cut into a flat strip and used with a rigid windlass (a stick or rod twisted to increase tension), but the fabric must be wide (>5 cm / 2 inches) and the windlass must be secured to prevent unwinding. A purpose-built tourniquet (e.g., CAT or SOFTT-W) is vastly preferable.

11. **Myth:** More washing equals longer garment life.
   **Reality:** Washing — particularly in hot water with harsh detergents, and tumble drying on high heat — is the primary driver of T-shirt degradation. Each wash cycle physically abrades fibres, fades dyes, and stresses seams. Cold water washing, gentle cycles, and air drying dramatically extend garment lifespan.

12. **Myth:** "Pre-shrunk" T-shirts will not shrink at all.
   **Reality:** Pre-shrunk treatment reduces, but does not eliminate, further shrinkage. Most pre-shrunk garments will still shrink an additional 3–5% over the first several wash-and-dry cycles, particularly if dried at high heat. Size up slightly when buying for long-term stockpile use.

13. **Myth:** Dark-coloured shirts are always hotter to wear in summer than white shirts.
   **Reality:** In direct sunlight, dark shirts do absorb more solar radiation at the outer surface. However, in desert or high-UV environments, loose-fitting dark or mid-tone shirts may actually provide better overall thermal protection by blocking UV from reaching skin, particularly compared to a thin white shirt which transmits significant UV. Fit, fabric weight, and breathability are as important as colour for thermal regulation.

14. **Myth:** A T-shirt used as a water pre-filter makes water safe to drink.
   **Reality:** Filtering water through a T-shirt removes visible particulates and some sediment, which helps improve the effectiveness of subsequent chemical or heat treatment — but it does not remove pathogens, dissolved chemicals, or microorganisms. Cloth filtration is a pre-treatment step only; purification (boiling, chemical treatment, or a rated filter) is always required.

## DIY / Manufacturing Guide

### Feasibility

**Can be made at home:** Yes (Partially — functional but not equivalent to industrial production)

### Instructions (if feasible)

15. **Select fabric.** Purchase jersey knit fabric (cotton or cotton-blend, 160–200 gsm recommended). Most fabric stores sell jersey by the metre. Calculate approximately 1.5–2 m for an adult size.
16. **Create or obtain a pattern.** Trace an existing well-fitting T-shirt onto pattern paper, adding a 1–1.5 cm seam allowance on all edges. Separate pattern pieces: front body, back body, two sleeves, and (optionally) a collar ribbing strip.
17. **Cut fabric.** Lay the jersey fabric folded double, align grainlines parallel to the stretch direction, and cut pieces using sharp fabric scissors or a rotary cutter and cutting mat.
18. **Sew shoulder seams.** With right sides together, sew front and back body pieces at the shoulder seams using a stretch stitch (zigzag or overlock). Press seams open or to one side.
19. **Attach collar (if using ribbing).** Fold the collar ribbing strip in half lengthwise, stretch slightly to fit the neckline, pin in quarters, and sew around the neck opening with a stretch stitch.
20. **Set in sleeves.** Match the sleeve cap to the armhole with right sides together, pin, and sew. Press seam toward sleeve.
21. **Sew side and sleeve seams.** Fold the assembled shirt so that sleeve and body side seams align. Sew from the sleeve cuff continuously down to the hem in one seam.
22. **Hem the cuffs and bottom.** Fold cuff and bottom hem edges by 1–2 cm, pin, and topstitch using a double-needle or twin-needle (for a professional stretchy hem) or a zigzag stitch.
23. **Inspect and press.** Turn right side out, check all seams for skipped stitches or puckering, and press lightly with a warm iron and a damp cloth.

**Required Materials:**
- Jersey knit fabric (1.5–2 m for an adult shirt)
- Ribbing fabric for collar (optional; approximately 25–30 cm × 6 cm)
- Thread: polyester or cotton/polyester blend (matched to fabric colour)
- Pattern paper or tracing paper
- Fabric scissors or rotary cutter

**Required Tools:**
- Sewing machine with stretch stitch capability (zigzag or dedicated stretch stitch)
- Overlock/serger machine (optional but strongly recommended for professional finish and seam durability)
- Double/twin needle (optional, for professional flat hems)
- Seam ripper
- Iron and ironing board
- Pins or fabric clips
- Tailor's chalk or fabric marker
- Measuring tape and ruler

**Estimated Time:** 2–5 hours for a first attempt; 1–2 hours for an experienced sewist.

**Safety Notes:**
- Keep fingers clear of the needle at all times. Use a seam guide or presser foot as a distance guide rather than fingertips.
- Jersey fabric tends to slip and stretch during cutting; use weights or pins to stabilise before cutting.
- Do not attempt serger use without reading the manual; the knife blade is exposed and extremely sharp.

### Why Not DIY (if not feasible)

Home production is entirely feasible for a functional garment. However, industrial T-shirt manufacturing offers advantages that are difficult to replicate at home: consistent seam tension, commercial-grade overlock stitching, preshrunk and colour-treated fabrics, and uniform sizing. For preparedness purposes, purchasing pre-made shirts in bulk is typically more cost-effective and time-efficient than home production unless fabric is available and sewing skills are already developed.
